This design-thinking exercise starts from a fact most people never think about: flushing is the single biggest water draw in an average household, according to the Bournemouth Water UK data the exercise opens with. From there it names three concrete problems worth designing for — water wastage from flushing, unhygienic conditions when a toilet area isn't kept clean, and clogging, which leads to overflow and the spread of germs. None of these are glamorous, which is rather the point of picking them.

## Observing before inventing: the AEIOU pass

Before jumping to solutions, the framework forces a structured look at the problem space across five lenses — Activities, Environments, Interactions, Objects, and Users. Applied here: the activities are flushing, hand-washing, and using a jet spray; the environments span household washrooms, public restrooms, and portable toilets; the objects are the commode, the seat, and the flush mechanism; and the users are, deliberately, everyone — plus municipal corporations as an institutional stakeholder with their own interest in water infrastructure. The interactions lens comes back almost empty, and that's an honest, useful observation in itself: a toilet is one of the few designed objects built around having essentially no interpersonal interaction at all.

## What already exists, and exactly where it falls short

The exercise doesn't skip past prior art. Low-flow toilets offer multiple flush settings — a half-flush for liquid waste, full for solid — but their weaker water force sometimes fails to fully clear the bowl, forcing a second flush that erases the water savings the feature was built for. Composting waterless toilets rely on natural decomposition and evaporation instead of water at all, but they demand real household maintenance discipline, and get unhygienic fast if that maintenance or the installation itself isn't done properly. Both are genuine trade-offs, not strawmen to wave away.

## Four features, built against the two problems that matter most

- **Controlled flush length** — bowl-displacement sensors, combined with how much toilet paper was dispensed, tune the flush's volume and duration to what's actually needed rather than a fixed amount every time.
- **Blockage detection** — a sensor along the U-bend halts flushing automatically before the bowl overflows, addressing the clogging problem directly rather than leaving it to a plunger after the fact.
- **Self-cleaning** — timers paired with weight sensors trigger a cleaning cycle between uses, aimed squarely at public restrooms where upkeep is inconsistent and largely out of any one person's control.
- **Fragrance** — an automatic post-use spray, framed as an incentive to make public restrooms more appealing to use rather than avoid.

## Being honest about the limits

The exercise doesn't oversell the concept. Installation cost is real and upfront, offset only gradually by water savings over time, and every one of the four features depends on electricity — a single failure mode that a purely mechanical toilet simply doesn't have. The future scope stays modest too: if adopted broadly across public restrooms and households, it could meaningfully cut water consumption, which was always the actual point, not the sensors themselves.
