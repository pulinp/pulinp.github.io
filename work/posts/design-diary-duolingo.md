Duolingo has served three different purposes for me at different times: extra practice as a student, prep before international trips, and building work-relevant language skills — all from the same app, in the pocket-sized moments of a commute. For this design diary entry, I used the same framework I'd applied to DoorDash: Aaron Walter's hierarchy of user needs, which argues a product has to clear functional, reliable, usable, and pleasurable bars — roughly in that order — before it earns real engagement.

## Level 1 — Functional

The core promise is a structured curriculum with interactive lessons and a gamified layer wrapped around it. That combination — breadth of language selection plus an intuitive teaching method — is most of why the app is as widely used as it is. It's not the only functional language app, but it makes the functional layer feel effortless rather than like homework.

## Level 2 — Reliable

Reliability here has less to do with uptime than with whether the app's *assessment* of you is accurate and consistent. Real-time feedback on exercises, accurate progress tracking, and — a detail I appreciated specifically — resurfacing the exact words you got wrong so you can check your progress against your own past mistakes. The gamified Progress Quiz reinforces the same idea in a lighter-weight format.

## Level 3 — Usable

Standard design patterns keep the interface predictable, but the usability decision that stood out to me was letting users choose their own starting proficiency level. It sounds minor, but it solves a real problem: a beginner and someone refreshing rusty language skills need completely different starting points, and forcing everyone through the same on-ramp would make the app less usable for a large share of its users.

## Level 4 — Pleasurable

Streaks, personalized goals, and gamification elements are the obvious layer here, but what actually registered as delight for me was the on-screen feedback and haptics at the moment of completing a lesson — a small celebratory beat that made finishing feel rewarding rather than just done. Combined with the ability to compete with friends or join language clubs, the app is clearly designed around making the *habit* pleasurable, not just the underlying learning.

## The takeaway

Duolingo is a strong illustration of why this framework orders its levels the way it does. The pleasurable layer — streaks, gamification, celebratory feedback — is the part most people associate with why Duolingo is "fun." But none of that would matter if the functional layer (an actually well-structured curriculum) and the reliable layer (accurate, trustworthy progress tracking) weren't solid underneath it. The gamification is memorable; the curriculum and tracking are what make the gamification worth returning to.
