Downtown Bryan Association runs the website that's supposed to connect visitors to the restaurants, clubs, and hotels in downtown Bryan, Texas — and it wasn't doing the job. This exercise takes on a full product canvas for "Easy Bryan," a rebuild aimed at one specific gap: nobody in the system, from a visitor trying to book a table to the association's own manager, could see real-time availability. The vision statement keeps it plain: *Easy Bryan aims to improve tourism by promoting local businesses and preserving culture and heritage.*

## Three people, three different reasons the current site fails them

- **Samantha Sharp**, 33, works remotely for a fintech startup and lives in Bryan. She's into live music and has clients who ask her about places to stay — she's tried to book restaurant tables and hotel rooms through the current system and found the process unintuitive, and she can't reliably find out when a show is scheduled. She's also the kind of user who'd pay for a membership if it got her priority booking.
- **Smith Reed**, 41, a business owner who's run restaurants in several U.S. cities before landing in Bryan. His problem is structural: the system doesn't show real-time table status, so seats that have emptied don't show as available, which is a direct loss of business, and he has no streamlined way to invite frequent visitors back.
- **Juan Porrello**, 31, the Easy Bryan manager with an MBA from University of Washington. He's stuck presenting crowd-volume and profit data to stakeholders without a dashboard, and as downtown's popularity grows week over week, he needs a way to actually manage a VIP membership program rather than track it informally.

## Ten features, two sprints, two developers

The canvas ties every feature back to one of those three people — visitor login and real-time availability for Samantha, seat management and discount coupons for Smith, daily summary reports and VIP-list tools for Juan. With only two developers on the project, the ten features split into two 15-working-day sprints:

| Sprint | Persona | User story | Priority | Points |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (Nov 7–25) | Visitor | Log in to manage personal bookings | High | 8 |
| 1 | Visitor | See real-time table/ticket availability | High | 10 |
| 1 | Visitor | Access the site on a phone | Low | 8 |
| 1 | Business owner | View business data to manage seating | Medium | 8 |
| 1 | Manager | See daily summary and visitor predictions | Medium | 14 |
| 2 (Nov 28–Dec 16) | Visitor | Purchase a membership | Medium | 8 |
| 2 | Business owner | Send discount coupons to loyalty members | High | 10 |
| 2 | Business owner | Send an in-app message to the manager | Low | 10 |
| 2 | Manager | Send newsletters and VIP invites to top members | High | 8 |
| 2 | Manager | Push update notifications to business owners | Medium | 14 |

The split isn't arbitrary — sprint one ships the features that make the system usable at all (login, live availability, a manager dashboard); sprint two layers on the retention and communication tools (membership, coupons, messaging) that only matter once the basics work.

## Success metrics that keep the canvas honest

The canvas sets five explicit targets rather than a vague "improve tourism": a 30% increase in tourist volume over a year, a 20% lift in customer satisfaction within six months, 50% more website traffic in a year, $100K in net financial gain, and the ability to handle 300% of regular traffic during peak season without degrading. Every wireframe that follows — login, home, restaurant detail, booking, analytics, profile — exists to move one of those five numbers, which is the actual point of building the canvas before touching a single screen.
