This one was a project-management coursework exercise built around Thompson Discount Mover, a moving company, using Microsoft Project to produce the kind of progress report a real project manager would hand a client or a steering committee. The assignment wasn't "describe the company" — it was "build the artifacts a functioning PM discipline actually requires," and each of those artifacts does a specific job that the others can't substitute for.

## Costs and the Work Breakdown Schedule

Before you can schedule or budget anything, you need the Work Breakdown Schedule: the project broken into the smallest pieces of work that can actually be assigned, estimated, and tracked. Everything downstream — the costs, the Gantt chart, the budget — inherits its structure from the WBS. Get the breakdown wrong (too coarse, and nothing is trackable; too granular, and the overhead of managing it swamps the work itself) and every other artifact inherits that mistake.

## Stakeholder analysis

A moving company project touches people with very different stakes in the outcome — the people whose belongings are being moved, the crews doing the physical work, whoever's paying for it, whoever's approving the budget. Stakeholder analysis exists to make those differences explicit before they become conflicts: who needs to be consulted, who just needs to be informed, and whose objection can actually stop the project versus whose can't.

## Gantt chart and PERT chart

These two look similar and get confused for each other, but they answer different questions. The Gantt chart is a timeline — what's happening when, and how tasks overlap, which is what makes it legible to a client glancing at a progress report. The PERT chart is a dependency map — which tasks can't start until another finishes, which is what actually determines the project's critical path and how much slack exists before a delay in one task becomes a delay in the delivery date. A report with only a Gantt chart tells you the schedule; a report with a PERT chart underneath it tells you why that schedule is defensible.

## Budget allocation and risk and change management

Maintaining budget allocation means tracking planned spend against actual spend by WBS category as the project runs, not just estimating once at the start. Risk management is the deliberate exercise of naming what could go wrong — a vehicle breakdown, a scheduling conflict, a damaged item — and having a response ready before it happens rather than improvising in the moment. Change management is the formal process for handling the scope changes that come up mid-project (a client adding a location, a timeline moving) without letting them quietly blow up the budget or the schedule that the WBS and Gantt chart were built around.

## Why bother with all eight

None of these artifacts is impressive on its own — a Gantt chart is just a timeline, a stakeholder analysis is just a list of names and interests. What the exercise actually teaches is that a progress report is only as trustworthy as the discipline behind it: costs that trace back to a real WBS, a schedule that accounts for real dependencies, and a risk plan that was written before the risk happened rather than after. That's the whole point of practicing it on something as unglamorous as a moving company — the discipline doesn't care whether the project is dramatic.
