The feature that actually got me onto GoodNotes was Apple Pencil support — the precision and responsiveness felt natural enough for real note-taking, not just sketching, whether I was annotating class material on my iPad or working through a diagram by hand. Since then it's become the place I keep notes across my iPad, iPhone, and MacBook, synced regardless of which device I picked up last. For this design diary entry, I ran GoodNotes through Aaron Walter's hierarchy of user needs to understand what's actually holding that experience together beyond the pencil input.

## Level 1 — Functional

The functional core is broader than "take notes" — it's note-taking, organization, and multimedia capture across devices, all searchable. Students and educators use it for class notes, professionals for meeting notes and shared project details, and creatives for more freeform capture. That range only works because the underlying data model (notebooks, tags, mixed content types) is flexible enough to support very different use cases without feeling like it's designed for none of them.

## Level 2 — Reliable

Reliability shows up as real-time sync, version history, and a seamless offline mode — the three things that matter most for a tool you're trusting with irreplaceable class or meeting notes. Version history in particular is a quiet vote of confidence: it means the app assumes you might need to undo something, rather than assuming every save is final.

## Level 3 — Usable

An intuitive interface, efficient search, and organizational primitives like notebooks, tags, and shortcuts make the difference between "a place notes go to be lost" and "a place notes go to be found again." Standard design patterns throughout mean there's very little relearning required even as your notebook collection grows large.

## Level 4 — Pleasurable

This is where the product-market fit with Apple Pencil really shows. Aesthetically pleasing design, customizable themes, and productivity tracking all contribute, but the pleasure layer is really carried by how the app *feels* to write in — collaborative sharing on top of that turns individual note-taking into something you can hand off or work through with someone else.

## The takeaway

GoodNotes is a good example of a product where the pleasurable layer (handwriting that feels natural) is also the acquisition hook — it's the reason people switch to the app in the first place. But the reason people *stay* is the boring, reliable layer underneath: sync that doesn't lose your notes, search that actually finds things, and organization that scales past a handful of notebooks. The pencil gets you in the door; the reliability is what keeps the door from mattering after that.
