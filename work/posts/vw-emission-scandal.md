This was a group case study — I was group lead on an eight-person team — built around one question: how does a company as established as Volkswagen end up systematically cheating emissions tests, and what actually happens once it's caught? "Dieselgate" is a good case for corporate ethics precisely because the fraud, the discovery, and the fallout are all well documented.

## What Volkswagen actually did

The mechanism was specific: Volkswagen installed software — a "defeat device" — that could detect when a car was undergoing an emissions test and altered engine performance accordingly, so the car ran clean on the test bench and dirty on the road. The motive traced back to a regulatory gap: US diesel emission standards were tougher than Europe's, and rather than build an engine that could meet them honestly, Volkswagen built software that could fake meeting them. The scandal only came to light because researchers from West Virginia University ran independent, on-road emissions tests — outside the controlled lab conditions the defeat device was designed to recognize — and found the discrepancy.

## The fallout, in dollars and lawsuits

The numbers escalate quickly once you lay out the timeline. Volkswagen sold roughly 480,000 affected vehicles in the US between 2009 and 2015. The EPA issued a Notice of Violation in September 2015, after finding the defeat devices had been in place across six consecutive model years. From there: a 2016 settlement of $15 billion covering a nationwide consumer class action and government civil enforcement, a 2017 criminal settlement of $2.8 billion in the US, and a 2018 settlement of $1.2 billion in Germany — a total cost north of $32 billion and still climbing at the time of the presentation, with SEC charges for defrauding bond investors and a separate class action in Germany still pending. India ran its own investigation through the Automotive Research Association of India, confirming by January 2017 that the same defeat-device software spanned three brands — Audi, Skoda, and Volkswagen — affecting roughly 3.4 lakh cars in the country alone, against 8.5 million in Europe and 500,000 in the US. Volkswagen committed to refitting up to 11 million vehicles worldwide.

## Environmental and health costs

The case study didn't stop at financial damage. Excess nitrogen oxide emissions contribute directly to acid rain, smog, and tropospheric ozone, and tie into broader climate warming. On health, the presentation cited an estimate of roughly 5,000 premature deaths linked to the excess pollution, driven by particulate matter and ozone exposure, alongside non-fatal effects — asthma, bronchitis, decreased lung function, and heart attacks tied to smog exposure.

## What changed afterward

Regulators fined Volkswagen and rewrote the rules. The EU formed a 28-nation oversight commission with the power to fine automakers roughly €30,000 per violating vehicle, introduced the more realistic WLTP testing standard, and passed legislation enabling post-approval testing, cross-border recalls, and mandatory disclosure of emissions-control software. India's National Green Tribunal fined Volkswagen ₹500 crore; the US collected $25 billion in fines tied to 580,000 tainted diesels; Germany fined the group over €1 billion; and Volkswagen separately committed $2.7 billion to environmental mitigation and $2 billion to clean-emissions infrastructure. On Volkswagen's side: testimony before the US Congress, a guilty plea, buyback and compensation offers to owners, and criminal charges against six executives. The case study's own conclusion is the right one to end on: this was a scandal that reshaped how emissions testing gets regulated, not a fine paid and forgotten, and it cost the company, its customers, and the environment in ways that didn't all show up on the same balance sheet.
