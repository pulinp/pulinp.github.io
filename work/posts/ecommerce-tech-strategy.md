For ISTM 643's Corporate Information Planning report, eight of us wrote one document: a strategy for how an e-commerce company should adopt emerging technology, with each person taking a topic off the Gartner Hype Cycle and writing a full chapter — initiatives, stakeholders, cost ranges, risks, change management. Mayur took the Metaverse, Risha took Blockchain, Prakarsh took Superapps, Tanishq took Generative AI, Archit took Web3, Vidhi took Augmented Reality, Madhu took machine learning code generation. I took Cloud Computing and Industry Cloud Platforms — the one chapter in the report that wasn't chasing a hype-cycle peak.

## Why the boring chapter still needs a real argument

It would have been easy to write the cloud chapter as an afterthought — "of course you need cloud infrastructure, that's just table stakes." Instead I broke it into three concrete initiatives, the same way every other chapter did: cloud-based data management (inventory and CRM systems that let a business track stock across locations and manage customer relationships without their own servers), business analytics and AI (real-time analysis of customer behavior and sales patterns at cloud scale), and cloud-based AR/VR (using cloud infrastructure to deliver the try-before-you-buy experiences other chapters were separately proposing, without every business needing to build that infrastructure itself). Each initiative got its own cost range and priority — data-integration work at $500K–$900K and "very high" priority, employee training at $500K–$700K and "low" priority — because a strategy report that doesn't put a number on its own recommendations isn't actually a strategy.

## The risk section is where a cloud chapter earns its keep

Cloud computing's risks aren't the same shape as blockchain's or the metaverse's, and the chapter treats them that way: cost of integration, the technical complexity of running multiple systems and platforms together, security exposure from storing data on third-party servers, competitive pressure as adoption becomes table stakes rather than differentiation, and platform lock-in constraints from whichever cloud provider a business picks. The change management section that follows is deliberately unglamorous too — clear goals, real employee training, a feedback loop, built-in contingencies, and ongoing measurement — because cloud adoption fails on organizational friction far more often than it fails on technology.

## What else landed on my desk

Past my own chapter, the report's conclusion needed pieces that tied all eight chapters together into one coherent recommendation, and those fell to me: the IT organization diagram, a written description of how that organization should be structured, and the Gantt chart sequencing all eight initiatives — metaverse, blockchain, superapps, ML code generation, AR, generative AI, web3, and cloud — against each other rather than letting each chapter pretend it would be the company's only priority.

## What the report argued, together

The conclusion's work-policy section is the part I find most honest about what these eight technologies actually demand of an organization: distributed teams need better remote-collaboration policy, employees need continuous upskilling just to keep pace, and — a detail that reads differently now than it did when we wrote it — the workforce needs training in writing prompts to get useful output from generative AI. Eight chapters, eight different technologies, one shared conclusion: none of this works without the unglamorous organizational scaffolding underneath it, which is exactly the position my own chapter had staked out from the start.
