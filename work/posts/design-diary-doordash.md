My first DoorDash order was actually for someone else — sending a birthday cake to a friend back in the US while I was still in India. Once I started my master's at Texas A&M, it became a weekly habit. For this design diary entry, right as I was about to place an order, it occurred to me that DoorDash was a good candidate to walk through Aaron Walter's hierarchy of user needs — the idea that a product has to clear functional, reliable, usable, and pleasurable bars, roughly in that order, before it earns real loyalty.

## Level 1 — Functional

The core job is simple to state: let people order food from a wide range of restaurants for delivery or pickup, all from one place. DoorDash clears this cleanly. Having every nearby restaurant under one roof — rather than juggling five different restaurant apps — is the entire value proposition, and it delivers on it without friction.

## Level 2 — Reliable

Reliability shows up as accurate orders, on-time delivery, and payments that just work. DoorDash backs this with real-time order tracking, delivery time estimates, and a two-sided rating system for drivers and restaurants — all of which build a visible layer of trust on top of the transaction. I've had orders arrive late or with the wrong item; both times, DoorDash resolved it with a refund and the option to get the correct item redelivered. That recovery path is arguably more important to trust than never having a problem in the first place.

## Level 3 — Usable

Usability here is about how little friction sits between "I'm hungry" and "food is on the way." Saved addresses, one-tap reorder, and photo-backed reviews and menu items all reduce the number of decisions and re-entries required. I tried the desktop site for this exercise specifically to compare, and it's not close — the mobile app is clearly the primary surface the product team optimizes for, and it shows.

## Level 4 — Pleasurable

This is the level that's easiest to skip and hardest to fake. DoorDash gets there through personalized recommendations based on order history and loyalty perks, but the detail that actually surprised me into a moment of delight was the package handoff feature — for a dollar or two, a driver already headed your way will drop off a FedEx or UPS package alongside your food. It's a small feature, but it's the kind of thing that turns "this app works" into "this app thought about my day."

## The takeaway

What makes this framework useful is less DoorDash's score on all four levels — plenty of apps clear those bars — and more how the levels build on each other: reliability doesn't matter if the core function doesn't work, and no amount of delight covers for an app that's unreliable. DoorDash's package-handoff feature only lands as delightful *because* the functional and reliable layers underneath it are already solid.
